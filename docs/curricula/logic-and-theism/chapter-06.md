# Chapter VI — Ultimate Reasons: Proofs *a contingentia mundi*

**Source:** Jordan Howard Sobel, *Logic and Theism*, Chapter VI, pp. 200–237  
**Status:** reviewed  
**Role in source-driven curriculum:** shifts from causal termination to explanation, necessity, and the structure of sufficient reasons

## Why this chapter matters for Externalize

Chapter VI looks superficially like a continuation of Chapter V: both ask why the World has the structure or existence it does and both seek something ultimate. But the underlying reasoning changes.

Chapter V asks whether causal chains terminate in first causes. Chapter VI allows that causal chains may be infinite and instead asks whether contingent reality has an **ultimate sufficient reason**.

This is therefore a good test of whether Externalize can teach the learner to distinguish:

- causal dependence;
- explanation;
- deductive entailment;
- modal necessity;
- evidential relevance;
- explanation of parts vs explanation of a whole.

The chapter also supplies an unusually strong example of **theory self-undermining through definition**: once Sobel explicates Leibnizian sufficient reasons as deductive reasons from necessities, the Principle of Sufficient Reason threatens to eliminate contingency altogether.

## VI.1 — From first causes to ultimate reasons

**Source focus:** Sobel motivates cosmological arguments as attempts to preserve the intuition behind first-cause reasoning while allowing infinite and beginningless causal histories. Even if every event has an earlier cause, one may still ask for a reason for the whole contingent order.

### Externalize sequence

1. Reuse a causal chain from Chapter V and extend it backward indefinitely.
2. Ask: `Does this chain have a first cause?` — no, by construction.
3. Ask separately: `Has every local event been causally accounted for?` — possibly yes.
4. Ask: `Has the existence of the whole chain been explained?` — now the learner confronts the new question.
5. Introduce a second edge type, `explains`, distinct from `causes`.

### Important curriculum finding

The same node-and-edge diagram should be able to switch **relation semantics**. `A → B` under `causes` and under `explains` are not interchangeable. Externalize should force the learner to label the relation before evaluating a regress.

**Reading exit:** learner can articulate why an infinite causal history does not by itself settle the cosmological-argument question Sobel is examining.

## VI.2 — Sobel’s Leibnizian cosmological argument

Sobel reconstructs the argument roughly as follows:

1. The World exists.
2. The World is contingent.
3. Everything that exists — facts and existent entities — has a sufficient reason.
4. The sufficient reason for a contingent entity ultimately runs in terms of an existent being.
5. Therefore an ultimate reason for the World exists, and that reason is a necessary being.

Two definitional principles do important work:

- a sufficient reason, as a reason “with which to stop,” ultimately starts from necessities;
- an entity at which such a sufficient reason starts is an ultimate reason for the thing explained.

### Externalize sequence

1. Present the five-step argument as a dependency graph, not prose.
2. Make each inference edge tappable to reveal what definition/licence it uses.
3. Ask which premise gets the argument from `World exists` to `World has a reason`.
4. Ask which premise gets from `reason` to `being`.
5. Ask why that being must be necessary rather than merely contingent.
6. Mark the conclusion explicitly as weaker than `God exists`: it yields an ultimate necessary being, not yet uniqueness or worship-worthiness.

### Likely stumbling blocks

- treating “ultimate reason” as synonymous with “God”;
- assuming a necessary being is automatically unique;
- confusing the thing that is a reason with the proposition/explanation in which it figures;
- assuming that because the argument is valid under certain definitions, its premises are mutually consistent.

**Reading exit:** learner can reconstruct the five-step argument and name the extra work still needed to reach God.

## VI.3 — What is “the World,” and what is a sufficient reason?

This section has two distinct curricular jobs.

### A. The World

Sobel uses `the World` for an aggregate of contingent entities together with contingent truths concerning them. It is not:

- the aggregate of absolutely everything;
- a possible world in the contemporary modal-logic sense;
- a necessary entity;
- automatically identical with a single “comprehensive contingent proposition.”

The World excludes necessary truths and necessary entities, including the necessary being the argument seeks.

### Externalize task: ontology sorter

Give cards for:

- contingent person;
- contingent event;
- necessary mathematical truth;
- possible world;
- the aggregate of contingent existents;
- God-as-necessary-being (hypothetical for classification only).

Ask which belong to Sobel’s `World` and why.

This is a strong **source-local terminology** example: Externalize must preserve Sobel’s special use of `World` without overwriting the canonical `possible-world` concept.

### B. Sufficient reasons

Sobel explicates a Leibnizian sufficient reason as much stronger than an ordinary explanation.

A sufficient reason:

- is complete/final;
- leaves no further “why?” unanswered;
- does not merely make its explanandum probable;
- necessitates the proposition explained;
- ultimately runs in terms of necessary truths/entities.

For his critical purposes, Sobel reduces such a reason to a one-premise deductive argument from a necessary truth to the explained fact/entity.

### Externalize sequence

1. Give a mundane explanation that raises another `why?`.
2. Extend it one layer.
3. Ask when the chain is supposed to stop.
4. Contrast:
   - `H makes E likely`;
   - `H entails E`.
5. Add modal labels to H: contingent vs necessary.
6. Ask what must be true of E if H is necessary and H entails E.

That last step should set up the core criticism before Sobel states it.

### Reading exit

Learner can distinguish ordinary sufficient-for-purpose explanation from the much stronger Leibnizian “sufficient reason.”

## VI.4 — Comparison with ontological and first-cause arguments

Sobel’s comparison is excellent for source-driven transfer.

### Ontological vs cosmological

Both can aim at a necessary being, but:

- ontological arguments aim to proceed without contingent premises;
- cosmological arguments explicitly use contingent premises, including the contingency of the World.

Also, a successful cosmological argument would not by itself establish the kind of perfect or unsurpassably great being that ontological arguments target.

### First-cause vs cosmological

Cosmological arguments can allow:

- infinite causal regresses;
- an eternal World;
- no first temporal cause inside the World.

They instead demand an ultimate reason beyond contingent reality.

### Externalize task: argument-family classifier

Give argument skeletons with labels removed. Ask whether each is:

- ontological;
- first-cause;
- ultimate-reason/cosmological;
- evidential.

Require the learner to justify classification by the **role of premises**, not theological vocabulary.

### Reading exit

Learner can distinguish these argument families structurally.

## VI.5 — Cleanthes and the whole/parts objection

Cleanthes’ objection is the familiar thought that once every part has been explained, asking for an explanation of the whole adds nothing.

Sobel’s response is nuanced: if each part has a genuinely **Leibnizian sufficient explanation**, then the whole may indeed be sufficiently explained by those explanations. But those sufficient explanations themselves must terminate outside the contingent cosmos in necessities. Thus the whole/parts objection does not by itself block the Leibnizian program.

### Externalize sequence

1. **Finite collection:** explain five machine components individually. Ask whether the functioning of the whole is thereby explained.
2. Vary whether component explanations are independent or jointly sufficient.
3. **Infinite chain:** give every node a predecessor explanation. Ask whether the explanations have a final stopping point.
4. Distinguish:
   - `every member has some explanation`;
   - `every member has a sufficient/ultimate explanation`;
   - `the aggregate has a sufficient explanation`.
5. Ask which implications hold under Sobel’s strong definition.

### Key distinction

Externalize should resist teaching a generic slogan such as “explaining the parts never explains the whole.” Sobel explicitly rejects that as too simple. Under his Leibnizian setup, sufficiently explaining every part can suffice for the whole; the real pressure is what counts as a sufficient explanation.

**Reading exit:** learner can state why Cleanthes’ objection is not Sobel’s main objection.

## VI.6 — The “small problem”: the premises are inconsistent

This is the chapter’s cleanest formal lesson.

Sobel’s reasoning:

1. A demonstration is a valid argument from necessary premises.
2. Therefore the conclusion of a demonstration must be necessary.
3. Leibnizian sufficient reasons, as Sobel has defined them, are demonstrations: necessary premises that entail their conclusions.
4. So only necessary truths/entities can have sufficient reasons of this kind.
5. Premise (3) says the World has a sufficient reason.
6. Therefore the World is necessary.
7. But premise (2) says the World is contingent.
8. The premises are inconsistent.

### Externalize sequence

1. Start with a world-card model: H true at all possible worlds.
2. Add `H entails E`.
3. Ask at which worlds E must be true.
4. Learner derives: all of them.
5. Therefore E is necessary.
6. Now label E as `the World exists` and compare with premise (2).

This is a perfect **derive-the-contradiction-before-Sobel** exercise.

### Mastery formalization

If `□H` and `□(H → E)`, then `□E`.

The app should expose that the result is not a controversial theological premise; it follows from the modal/deductive structure once sufficient reason is defined this strongly.

### Reading exit

Learner can explain why `contingent + sufficient reason from necessities` is unstable.

## VI.7 — The larger problem: explanatory principles can erase contingency

Sobel generalizes the local inconsistency into a more ambitious criticism.

The Principle of Sufficient Reason, understood in the strong Leibnizian sense, produces a **modal collapse**: if every truth/existent has such a sufficient reason, every truth/existent becomes necessary.

He then argues that even a weaker principle requiring a true-premised deductive explanation for every contingency can collapse contingency if circular explanations are excluded.

### Externalize sequence

1. Give a set of world cards with one contingent proposition `C` true here but false elsewhere.
2. Ask learner to supply a necessary explanans that entails C.
3. Show that any such explanans forces C into every world.
4. Therefore no such explanans can exist while C remains contingent.
5. Repeat with a contingent chain of true explanantia and forbid circularity.
6. Ask whether the chain can explain *all* contingencies without leaving a brute contingency somewhere.

### Cross-chapter reuse

This should link explicitly back to Chapter IV’s modal collapse. Same high-level failure label, different mechanism:

- Gödelian theory: axioms about positive properties/essence yield collapse;
- Leibnizian explanatory ambition: universal sufficient-reason principle yields collapse.

Externalize should make this a **concept recurrence**, not two separate lessons.

### Reading exit

Learner can explain why a principle intended to explain contingencies may paradoxically deny that there are any contingencies.

## VI.8 — Can everything contingent have some reason at all?

Sobel presses further than the Leibniz-specific objection. He argues toward the stronger conclusion that if there are contingencies, then not everything contingent can have a reason of some sort; there must be at least one brute, inexplicable contingency.

A key idea is what he calls the `World of Limited Contingencies`: the aggregate of contingencies. If that aggregate itself is contingent, and a complete reason for it would have to be demonstrative, then it cannot have such a complete reason.

Sobel’s coda is intentionally radical: if there are contingencies, the demand for a reason for **all** of them is not merely unmet but impossible to satisfy on his argument.

### Externalize sequence

1. Create a finite set of contingent facts and allow explanations among them.
2. Prevent cycles.
3. Ask whether the directed explanation graph can have every node explained by another node inside the set.
4. For finite DAGs, a source node must remain.
5. Generalize beyond finite sets with Sobel’s stronger argument about the aggregate.
6. Introduce `brute contingency` as a possible endpoint, not as a failure of the exercise.

### Important caution

The finite-DAG intuition is only a teaching bridge. Sobel’s section is stronger and more general; the app should not silently substitute the finite graph result for his philosophical argument.

**Reading exit:** learner understands the shape of Sobel’s claim that universal explanatory completeness is incompatible with contingency.

# Postscript — necessities and contingencies

Sobel argues for a sharp divide between necessities and contingencies. Necessary truths can assist calculation or presentation, but, in his view, a necessity such as `5 + 7 = 12` does not itself explain a contingent fact such as why a particular person happens to have €12 in two pockets.

He also stresses that necessary beings, existing in every possible world, cannot by themselves probabilistically favor one contingent cosmos over another.

### Externalize exercise family: relevance classifier

Given H and E, classify H as:

- deductively entails E;
- makes E more probable;
- merely helps calculate E from other facts;
- explanatorily relevant;
- irrelevant.

Use both necessary and contingent H.

This prepares Chapter VII, where probabilistic explanatory relevance becomes central.

# Appendices

## Appendix A — Leibniz’s problem with necessity

Useful as a **modal-collapse repair workshop**.

Leibniz wants:

- God necessary;
- the created World contingent;
- God’s choice of the best world rationally explained;
- yet God’s choice free rather than necessitated.

These pressures generate a trilemma around necessity, explanation, and freedom.

### Candidate Externalize task

Represent the commitments as toggles. Let the learner see which combinations imply that the created world becomes necessary or that God’s choice lacks a sufficient reason.

## Appendix B — Leslie’s axiarchism

Useful as transfer for the assumption that an ultimate explanation must terminate in a **being**. A non-agentive value-based explanation can be used to test whether premise (4) is doing hidden theological work.

## Appendix C — Koons’s “New Look” cosmological argument

Treat as advanced transfer. The appendix’s “start-up” and “terminal” problems can be mapped against the generic framework:

```text
start-up: what licenses the explanatory demand?
terminal: what licenses the character/uniqueness/religious adequacy of the terminus?
```

# Architectural findings from Chapter VI

1. **Externalize needs typed explanatory relations, not just typed causal relations.** `causes`, `entails`, `makes-probable`, and `explains` must remain distinct.
2. **Source-local terminology must coexist with canonical concepts.** Sobel’s `World` is not the canonical `possible-world` node.
3. **Definitions can generate contradictions with premises.** The curriculum engine should allow a definition to be an active dependency, not passive glossary text.
4. **Cross-source recurrence matters.** `modal-collapse` should be one durable concept reused from Gödel to Leibniz.
5. **Negative knowledge is curriculum-worthy.** Learning that no explanation of a stipulated kind can exist is a genuine capability, not merely failure to find one.
6. **A source route can prepare the next source section.** The necessity/probability distinction at the end of VI leads naturally into Chapter VII’s Bayesian treatment of explanatory hypotheses.

# Validation target

At Reading depth, the learner should be able to answer, without the book:

1. How is an ultimate-reason argument different from a first-cause argument?
2. What does Sobel mean by a Leibnizian sufficient reason?
3. Why does a necessary premise that entails a proposition make that proposition necessary?
4. Why does this create trouble for the claim that the World is contingent?
5. Why is Cleanthes’ whole/parts objection not Sobel’s decisive criticism?
6. What does it mean to say the Principle of Sufficient Reason causes modal collapse?

If the learner can derive the contradiction in §6 from world semantics before Sobel gives it, Externalize has succeeded at the chapter’s central pedagogical task.
