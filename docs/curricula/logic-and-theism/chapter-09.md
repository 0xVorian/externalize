# Chapter IX — Romancing the Stone

**Source:** Jordan Howard Sobel, *Logic and Theism*, Chapter IX, pp. 345–368  
**Status:** reviewed  
**Role in source-driven curriculum:** uses modal scope, essential properties, self-modification, and attribute compatibility to debug omnipotence paradoxes

## Why this chapter matters for Externalize

Chapter IX is a particularly strong test of **concept reuse across a source route**. The learner should already have encountered:

- possibility and necessity in Chapter III;
- essential properties in Chapters I, III, and IV;
- wide/narrow scope ambiguity in Chapter II;
- conclusion-strength and argument debugging throughout Chapters V–VI.

Sobel now combines these pieces in a domain where ordinary language makes them unusually easy to conflate: claims about what an agent **can do**, what it **does**, what it **can cease to be**, and what it **must remain across possible worlds**.

The chapter’s main pedagogical lesson is therefore broader than omnipotence:

> **A capacity claim must be indexed to the agent’s state, modal scope, and the semantics of the task.**

A paradox can appear merely because those dimensions were left implicit.

# IX.1 — “Common names” of God

**Source focus:** Sobel begins from traditional divine attributes and turns specifically to omnipotence as part of the common conception of God. The larger chapter asks not only whether omnipotence itself is coherent, but whether it can coexist with attributes commonly added to it.

## Externalize mode

**Interrogate / concept separation.**

Present the traditional attribute bundle as independently toggleable claims rather than one fused `perfect God` object:

```text
omnipotent
omniscient
essentially omniscient
perfectly good
essentially perfectly good
necessary existent
necessarily everlasting
```

Ask the learner to predict whether adding an apparently stronger attribute can ever make the total package **less** coherent.

This prepares the core surprise of the chapter: ordinary omnipotence is, on Sobel’s account, possible, while some strengthened combinations are not.

# IX.2 — What should “omnipotent” mean?

Sobel starts from the intuitive thought that an omnipotent can do anything that can be done, but immediately encounters problems with the way tasks are described.

## Source-derived distinctions

### Logically impossible tasks do not count against omnipotence

Making `2 + 2 = 5` is not something an omnipotent must be able to do, because it is not logically possible for **any** agent to do it.

### Self-referential task descriptions are dangerous

A formulation such as `lift a stone that one does not lift` can make a perfectly ordinary capacity look contradictory, because successful performance changes the truth of the description.

### Universal actions

Sobel therefore introduces **universal actions**: actions whose performance does not essentially involve a particular individual as agent, subject, or otherwise.

His developing definition compares what b can do with what it is logically possible that **some being** can do, with a further qualification designed to exclude cases where b’s performance would instantiate an absolutely impossible action.

## Externalize sequence

1. **Task coherence:** classify:
   - lift 100 kg;
   - square the circle in Euclidean geometry;
   - make 2 + 2 equal 5;
   - lift a stone that one has not lifted;
   - make oneself unable to lift a stone.
2. Ask whether failure to perform each task shows weakness.
3. **Normalize:** rewrite person-specific/self-referential tasks into universal actions when possible.
4. Show the difference between:
   - `nobody can do A`;
   - `this agent cannot do A`.
5. Introduce the omnipotence definition only after the learner can classify the task semantics.

## Likely stumbling blocks

- treating a grammatically well-formed task as a logically possible task;
- assuming all `cannot` claims concern limitations of power;
- letting a task’s description change truth conditions during its own performance;
- confusing a universal action with a proposition mentioning a particular agent.

## Reading exit

Learner can explain why omnipotence should not require doing the logically impossible and why task individuation matters to a coherent definition.

# IX.3 — “Essential properties?”

This section is philosophically important because Sobel does **not** merely assume that essentialized versions of ordinary attributes are straightforward.

## Source-derived focus

Sobel distinguishes familiar essential properties he finds intelligible — for example, being an animate being for a person or being a building for Buckingham Palace — from more puzzling proposed essential properties such as being essentially unilingual, essentially sighted, essentially honest, essentially omniscient, or essentially omnipotent.

His worry is not simply that the phrases contain a formal contradiction. He doubts what could ground such very specific essential properties in the nature of their bearer, and he questions what epistemic grounds we could have for their possibility. Merely having coherent-looking words is not enough.

He notes a modal consequence useful for later reasoning: given that Margaret exists, the possibility that she is **essentially unilingual** requires that she is actually unilingual. Essential-property possibility therefore has stronger actual-world constraints than an ordinary possibility claim.

## Externalize sequence

1. **Property ladder:** classify properties as:
   - actual only;
   - plausibly essential;
   - claimed essential but puzzling;
   - obviously impossible.
2. Use world cards for one person:
   - exists and P;
   - exists and ¬P;
   - does not exist.
3. Ask what must be removed from the model for `essentially P` to hold.
4. Compare:
   - `possibly P`;
   - `possibly essentially P`.
5. Ask what the latter implies about the actual individual under the S5-style framework being used.
6. Keep a visible distinction between:
   - **formal consistency of words**;
   - **modal possibility**;
   - **evidence for possibility**.

## Architectural finding

Externalize needs more than a boolean `possible/impossible` label. It should support **epistemic status of modal claims**:

```text
formally represented
no contradiction yet found
source asserts possibility
source doubts possibility
derived possible/impossible in current system
```

This prevents “we can symbolize it” from silently becoming “it is possible.”

## Reading exit

Learner understands why adding `essentially` is not a harmless intensifier and why modal possibility itself can require argument.

# IX.4 — Is omnipotence possible? Recasting the stone paradox

This is the chapter’s central concrete example.

## Source-derived argument

A familiar paradox can be articulated:

1. Necessarily, if a being is omnipotent, it can make a stone it cannot lift.
2. Necessarily, if a being is omnipotent, it cannot make a stone it cannot lift.
3. Therefore necessarily there is no omnipotent being.

Sobel’s diagnosis focuses on premise (2) after stripping the outer necessity. The sentence

> If a being is omnipotent, then it cannot make a stone that it cannot lift

has two modal-scope readings.

### Wide modal scope

It is impossible that a being **is omnipotent and makes** a stone it cannot lift.

This is compatible with Sobel’s definition: at the time the being has made an unliftable stone, it would no longer be omnipotent.

### Narrow modal scope

For any being that is omnipotent, it is impossible for that being to make such a stone.

This is the reading needed for the paradox, but Sobel rejects it.

## Why ordinary omnipotence survives

On Sobel’s account, an omnipotent can create a stone it cannot lift by also diminishing its own lifting power. In doing so it **relinquishes omnipotence**. Therefore:

- no being can both remain omnipotent and have an unliftable stone;
- but a currently omnipotent being can perform an act after which it is no longer omnipotent.

The paradox equivocates between those claims.

## Externalize sequence

1. Show an agent at world/time `w0`: `O = true`.
2. Give action `make stone + diminish lifting power`.
3. Move to `w1`: `O = false`, stone exists, stone not liftable by agent.
4. Ask:
   - Was the action available at `w0`?
   - Is the agent omnipotent at `w1`?
5. Render the two scope readings as nested boxes.
6. Ask which version the paradox needs.
7. Let the learner repair the argument by choosing a reading; show whether validity or premise truth fails.

## High-value generalization

Use non-theological analogues:

- a person with authority to resign;
- an administrator able to revoke their own admin rights;
- a process capable of terminating itself;
- a sovereign able to abolish an office it currently occupies.

The reusable concept is **power to relinquish a power/status**.

## Reading exit

Learner can explain why Sobel thinks ordinary omnipotence is possible and why the stone paradox depends on a modal-scope mistake.

# IX.5 — Essential omnipotence

Here the result changes.

## Source-derived contrast

An **essentially omnipotent** being cannot simply use its omnipotence to relinquish omnipotence while continuing to exist. If it exists in a possible world, it must remain omnipotent there.

Sobel’s second stone therefore dramatizes the general incapacity of an essential omnipotent to relinquish its omnipotence.

The appendix articulates the resulting argument:

1. If a being is essentially omnipotent, it must be possible for it to make a stone it cannot lift.
2. If a being is essentially omnipotent, it cannot possibly make such a stone.
3. Therefore there is no essentially omnipotent being.

Unlike the ordinary-omnipotence paradox, Sobel holds the crucial second premise to be true here.

## Externalize sequence

1. Reuse the successful ordinary-omnipotence world transition from §4.
2. Toggle `omnipotence = essential`.
3. Attempt the same transition.
4. The target world now violates the essential-property constraint if the same individual still exists there.
5. Ask whether the being could instead cease to exist.
6. Introduce Sobel’s “ontological suicide” near-miss for an essential omnipotent that is not necessary: the being disappears and a nearly identical non-omnipotent successor-like being remains.
7. Ask whether that counts as **the same being** having relinquished omnipotence. It does not.

## Architectural finding

Externalize needs **identity-preserving transitions** in world diagrams. It is not enough to show property values by world; the learner must see whether the same individual persists across the transition.

## Reading exit

Learner can state exactly why the relinquishment solution available to ordinary omnipotence is unavailable to essential omnipotence.

# IX.6 — Essential omnipotence plus necessary everlasting existence

Sobel notes that the impossibility becomes especially transparent when essential omnipotence is combined with necessary everlasting existence.

Such a being cannot:

- cease to be omnipotent while existing, because omnipotence is essential;
- escape the constraint by ceasing to exist, because it exists necessarily and everlastingly.

The stone-making route is therefore blocked from both directions.

## Externalize interaction: constraint composition

Represent attributes as constraints on transitions:

```text
essential omnipotence
  -> if same being exists, O must remain true

necessary everlasting existence
  -> same being must continue to exist
```

Now ask whether the target state `same being exists & ¬O` is reachable.

This is a reusable **constraint-composition** exercise family and a natural bridge to SAT/model-checking style interactions later.

# IX.7 — Omnipotence with other essential attributes

Sobel argues that even ordinary omnipotence conflicts with certain other attributes when those attributes are essential.

## Source-derived examples

### Essential goodness / honesty

An essentially honest or perfectly good being cannot lie in any world in which it exists. Yet lying is an action some beings can perform. On Sobel’s omnipotence definition, that inability conflicts with omnipotence.

### Essential omniscience

An essentially omniscient being cannot deceive itself. Again, self-deception is something some beings can do, so the essential incapacity conflicts with omnipotence as Sobel defines it.

### General form

Omnipotence cannot be combined with an essentialized attribute **when a being possessing that attribute nonessentially could rid itself of it through an action**.

## Externalize sequence

1. Build an attribute compatibility matrix.
2. Columns: actions such as `lie`, `self-deceive`, `suicide`, `relinquish power`.
3. Rows: omnipotent, essentially honest, essentially omniscient, necessarily everlasting.
4. For each cell ask whether the action is:
   - logically impossible for anyone;
   - possible for some being;
   - incompatible only with this being’s essential attribute.
5. Highlight contradiction only when all relevant claims are jointly asserted.

## Important pedagogical distinction

`cannot lie because lying is logically impossible` is very different from `cannot lie because this being is essentially good`.

The first does not limit omnipotence under Sobel’s definition. The second does, because lying remains a universal action possible for some agents.

## Reading exit

Learner can explain why strengthening another divine attribute can reduce the set of actions open to the bearer and thereby conflict with omnipotence.

# IX.8 — What is left for God of omnipotence?

Sobel’s answer is not that God-like power must be trivial.

He introduces the deliberately awkward label **ONSLIP** — an “only necessarily self-limited power.” Roughly, such a being can perform every generally possible task except those barred by:

1. its own essential attributes; and
2. necessary everlasting existence, if it has that condition.

Thus an essentially perfectly good being need not be able to lie; an essentially omniscient being need not be able to deceive itself; a necessarily everlasting being need not be able to commit suicide.

Sobel explicitly says this is **not** merely a disguised redefinition of omnipotence. An ONSLIP can be far less than omnipotent if it has severely limiting essential features. The category instead captures the maximal power compatible with whatever necessary self-constraints the being has.

## Externalize sequence

1. Give a being’s essential-attribute profile.
2. Generate the universe of otherwise possible universal actions.
3. Filter actions inconsistent with the profile.
4. The remaining set is the being’s admissible maximal capability set.
5. Ask whether that set qualifies as omnipotence under Sobel’s strict definition or only as ONSLIP-level power.

## Architectural finding

Capabilities should be generated **relative to constraints**, not stored as an absolute checklist. This is a much more general model for powers, permissions, and affordances.

## Reading exit

Learner can distinguish:

- omnipotence;
- essential omnipotence;
- very extensive but necessarily self-limited power.

# Architectural findings from Chapter IX

1. **Tasks/actions need semantics of their own.** A string description is not a sufficient task identity.
2. **Modal scope should be rendered structurally.** The stone paradox is a direct analogue of Chapter II’s scope problems, now with `□/◇` rather than descriptions/negation.
3. **World models need trans-world identity.** Essential-property reasoning depends on whether the same individual persists.
4. **Capabilities are state-dependent.** `can A now` and `can become a state in which A is no longer possible` must remain distinct.
5. **Attribute bundles should be constraint systems.** The curriculum should support compatibility testing among separately mastered concepts.
6. **“Possible” needs provenance/status.** Formal intelligibility, no-known-contradiction, and established possibility are different claims.
7. **A source can reuse old skills in a radically different surface domain.** Modal scope learned through ontological arguments becomes the mechanism for dissolving the stone paradox.

# Validation target

At Reading depth, the learner should be able to answer without the text:

1. Why does failure to perform a logically impossible task not count against omnipotence?
2. Why does Sobel think an ordinary omnipotent can make a stone it cannot lift?
3. What is the wide-scope/narrow-scope ambiguity in the paradox?
4. Why does the relinquishment solution fail for **essential** omnipotence?
5. Why does necessary everlasting existence make the constraint tighter still?
6. Why can essential goodness or essential omniscience conflict with omnipotence on Sobel’s definition?
7. What is an ONSLIP intended to capture, and why is it not simply a synonym for omnipotence?

If the learner can debug the stone paradox by moving a single modal-scope boundary and then predict why essential omnipotence changes the result, Externalize has successfully transferred modal reasoning from Chapters II–IV into a new conceptual domain.
